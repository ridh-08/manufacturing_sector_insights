Engineering Goods and Machinery: Value-Adding at Home, Precision-Dependent Abroad

Unlike India's assembly-heavy sectors, Engineering Goods genuinely adds value domestically, but the sector still imports nearly twice what it exports, concentrated in the high-precision machine categories India's overwhelmingly MSME-scale manufacturing base has not yet built.
Primary Research: Dhairya Shah Compiled by: Riddhi Bhargava Institute of Manufacturing and Economy
In brief. Engineering Goods and Machinery (NIC Division 28) GVO grew 181% between 2014-15 and 2023-24, with value-added intensity (GVA/GVO) holding in a relatively stable 21-25% band throughout, confirming this is a genuinely value-adding engineering industry rather than an assembly-based one [1]. Concentration has stayed Competitive throughout the decade (HHI around 590-870, never above the Moderate threshold), reflecting a market structure dominated by large numbers of small and medium engineering firms rather than a few large players [5]. Trade tells a more difficult story: exports of ₹2,37,173 crore against imports of ₹4,32,288 crore leave a deficit of roughly ₹1,95,115 crore, nearly 1.8 times exports, concentrated overwhelmingly in one product line (HS 8479, machinery not elsewhere classified) sourced overwhelmingly from one country, China (43.7% of all machinery imports) [3]. The Rajkot cluster in Gujarat, this paper's focus for cluster interconnectedness, illustrates the sector's structure especially well: it is not one cluster but an integrated ecosystem spanning foundries, bearings, machine tools, and final pump assembly, appearing under three separate sector tags (Engineering Goods, Metals/Foundry, Electrical Equipment/Motors) in the compiled cluster register [9].
§1

The Scale

Engineering Goods and Machinery's factory base grew from 12,281 in 2014-15 to 14,393 in 2023-24 (+17.2%), while Gross Value of Output (GVO) grew far faster, from ₹2.73 lakh crore to ₹7.68 lakh crore (+181.3%) [1]. ASI-measured employment (Employees, Total) rose from 7,08,412 to 12,50,282 (+76.5%) over the same period.

Value-added intensity (GVA as % of GVO) held in a 21-25% band across the decade, from 24.2% in 2014-15 to 22.3% in 2023-24, with a dip to 20.97% in 2022-23 [1]. This is a materially different pattern from the Electronics sector snapshot, where the equivalent ratio fell from 19.4% to 13.2%. None of the five leading states shows material intensity comparable to electronics assembly, where the value of purchased materials dominates output value: this is a genuinely value-adding engineering industry, not an assembly-based one [8].

The Recovery

The IIP for Engineering Goods rose steadily from 102.9 (2012-13) to an early peak of 123.4 in 2018-19, then fell through 2019-20 and 2020-21, bottoming at 92.5, before recovering through the 2020s to a new high of 133.3 in 2025-26, which exceeds the pre-COVID peak, a genuinely completed recovery rather than the still-incomplete one seen in the Electronics sector snapshot [2].

§3

The Gap

Trade figures in this section are drawn from a dedicated Engineering Goods and Machinery trade analysis (NIC Division 28 scope) rather than the DGCI&S TradeStat-based dataset used in the Electronics sector snapshot. Data note The DGCI&S-based "Engineering Goods" cut used in the Electronics paper shows materially different totals (Export ₹2.04 lakh crore, Import ₹3.64 lakh crore, Import Dependency 64.1%), reflecting a narrower product scope than the full NIC Division 28 basis used in the dedicated analysis below. The NIC Division 28 figures are used here as the more complete and better cross-verified basis (they are independently corroborated against EEPC India and IBEF published figures, see below), but the discrepancy is flagged rather than silently resolved [3],[9].

On the NIC Division 28 basis, exports stood at ₹2,37,173 crore against imports of ₹4,32,288 crore, a trade deficit of roughly ₹1,95,115 crore, about 1.8 times exports [9]. This is the sector that supplies the pumps, valves, gears and industrial machinery every other factory depends on, and India currently sources more of it from abroad than it sells to the world.

Exports are genuinely dispersed: the top five destinations (USA, UAE, Germany, China, Saudi Arabia) account for 39.5% of exports, just under the "dispersed trade" threshold, and this is corroborated by EEPC India's independently tracked figure that the top five importing nations account for 36% of India's total engineering exports in FY26 [9]. The one notable concentration is the USA alone at roughly 21% of exports, more than the next three countries combined.

Imports tell a one-country story. The top five import origins (China, Germany, Japan, Italy, USA) account for 70.4% of imports, and within that, China alone supplies 43.7% of all of India's machinery imports, more than four times Germany, the second-largest source [9]. This is corroborated by Carnegie Endowment analysis showing machinery was one of just four product groups (with electronics, organic chemicals, and plastics) that made up nearly 80% of India's imports from China in the first ten months of 2025, at roughly $26 billion (≈₹2.2 lakh crore) for machinery alone.

The trade pattern is not simple substitution but genuine intra-industry trade: India exports one grade or type of valve, gear, or bearing while importing a different, typically more precision-intensive, grade of the same HS category. HS 8481 (valves) and HS 8483 (gears, bearings, transmission shafts) both show this pattern, standard industrial-grade Indian output coexisting with imported high-precision variants [9]. The clearest exception is HS 8479 (machinery not elsewhere classified), the single largest import line at ₹36,559 crore with no meaningful export counterpart at all, the trade-data signature of a genuine capability gap rather than a quality-tier split. This lines up with an academic finding that India's domestic machine tool industry holds only around 45% of the domestic market, concentrated in less-advanced product categories, while high-end machine tools remain import-dependent [9]. Two structural constraints are cited for why this persists: over 60% of Indian SMEs lacked the financial capacity to invest in automated machinery systems as of 2023, and under 25% of MSMEs have adopted advanced digital tools like CNC and automation, a self-reinforcing gap given MSMEs account for roughly 45% of India's industrial production [9].

§4

The Structure

Cost, capital, and returns indicators in this section are sourced from CMIE Industry Outlook on an aggregate basis (all companies, listed and unlisted) [4].

Sales grew from ₹25,10,270 million in 2014-15 to ₹51,38,372 million in 2024-25 (+104.7%). Raw Material Cost as a share of Sales was broadly stable, 45.9% to 47.5%, while Employee Cost fell from 12.2% to 10.8%, a far more modest cost-structure shift than seen in Electronics' sharp rise in material intensity [4].

4.1 Returns, Leverage and Liquidity

The sector's returns profile shows a marked turnaround from a weak starting point: ROA was actually negative in 2014-15 (-0.22%) and ROE was negative (-0.57%), before recovering to 6.08% and 12.25% respectively by 2024-25. ROCE rose from 12.76% to 18.82%, and PAT Margin rose from a thin 0.90% to 8.23% [4].

§5

The Firms

Concentration in this sector has stayed in the Competitive band (HHI below 1,500) throughout the available series, falling further from 868 in 2015-16 to 588 in 2024-25 [5], consistent with a market structure genuinely spread across large numbers of MSME-scale engineering firms rather than dominated by a handful of large players, the same structural signature visible in the Rajkot cluster discussion in Section 8.

The top-20 firm sales ranking is markedly more stable than Electronics' churn-heavy leaderboard: Siemens Ltd. holds rank 1 in most years (₹10,740 crore in FY14 to ₹20,250 crore in FY25), and JCB India has held rank 2-4 throughout, rising to ₹19,692 crore by FY25 [6]. The clearest relative decliner is Tractors & Farm Equipment Ltd. (TAFE), which held rank 2 in FY14 (₹6,259 crore) but slipped to rank 14 by FY25 (₹7,930 crore), growing only 26.7% over a decade in which the leaders roughly doubled or tripled, a stagnation rather than a collapse.

A tighter, higher margin band than Electronics. Three-year average (FY22-23 to FY24-25) PAT margins across the top firms range from 32.9% (Suzlon Energy) down to 1.4% (Amber Enterprises India) [7], still a wide spread but nowhere near as extreme as Electronics' 50-fold dispersion, and the firms occupying this list are overwhelmingly established capital-goods manufacturers, Cummins, Bosch, Siemens, JCB, Caterpillar, John Deere, rather than recent contract-assembly entrants. This is consistent with the sector-wide finding that Engineering Goods is a genuinely value-adding industry: even its lower-margin firms (Amber Enterprises at 1.4%, Hitachi Astemo at 6.7%) are components and precision-engineering manufacturers, not pure assemblers.
§6

The Support

Unlike Electronics, this sector does not have a dedicated Production Linked Incentive scheme; instead, the relevant national policy is the National Capital Goods Policy, first issued in 2016. The 2016 policy aimed to raise the domestic production share of India's capital goods demand but did not hit its target, a shortfall the Ministry of Heavy Industries' own working-group report flags explicitly. A draft National Capital Goods Policy 2025 has been floated as a successor, but its outcomes are not yet observable in the data available to this paper [8].

The Ministry of Heavy Industries' own working-group report on the capital goods sector had flagged the import-dependency trajectory years in advance: capital goods imports grew at 9.8% per annum, with the import share of the domestic capital goods market rising from 34% in 2009-10 to 40% in 2014-15, described in the government's own words as "indicating a looming threat to India's self-reliance and national security" [8]. India's machine tool industry specifically remains small in global terms: production reached an estimated ₹13,571 crore in FY2023-24 (+10% YoY) against consumption of ₹27,265 crore (+11% YoY), with machine tool imports at ₹15,352 crore against exports of just ₹1,659 crore [8]. Investment pipeline activity for the sector overall is comparatively modest and has not grown the way Electronics' has: National Investments Outstanding stood at ₹30,348 crore in 2014-15, ₹25,810 crore in 2024-25, and ₹27,869 crore in 2025-26 [8], essentially flat over a decade rather than the near-tenfold rise seen in Electronics, a pipeline signature consistent with a mature, MSME-dominated sector rather than one in a build-out phase.

Where It Is Made: States

Maharashtra leads Engineering Goods and Machinery GVO in 2023-24 at ₹18.71 lakh crore, followed by Tamil Nadu (₹13.74 lakh crore), Gujarat (₹13.62 lakh crore), Karnataka (₹5.66 lakh crore) and Haryana (₹5.02 lakh crore) [1].

Gujarat and Tamil Nadu grew faster than the leader, Maharashtra, over the decade (+191.8% and +187.6% vs. +152.7%), narrowing the gap even though Maharashtra retains the top rank on both GVA and GVO. Gujarat's structure reflects its wide spread of MSMEs producing pumps, valves, compressors and general-purpose machinery in Rajkot, Ahmedabad, Vadodara and Bharuch, in contrast to Maharashtra and Tamil Nadu, where machinery production concentrates in larger integrated engineering plants [8]. Gujarat also posted the highest Net Fixed Capital Formation of the five states in 2023-24 (₹4,559 crore), read here as a transition from medium-scale engineering toward more capital-intensive manufacturing, tracking the post-2021-22 investment surge visible nationally.

8. Where It Is Made: Clusters

Rajkot is this paper's focus cluster, and it is best understood not as a single-product cluster but as an integrated engineering ecosystem, a structural pattern distinct from most other clusters in the register. Rajkot appears under three separate sector tags in the compiled Cluster Register, reflecting three genuinely interlinked sub-industries operating in the same district: the Rajkot Engineering Cluster (NIC 28, rank 1 of the sector's registered clusters, ~2,190 units, ₹5,450 crore turnover), the Rajkot Foundry Cluster (Metals, NIC 24/25, 2,380-2,480 units, ₹5,400 crore turnover), and Rajkot Electric Motor (Electrical Equipment, NIC 271) [9].

Rajkot Engineering & Pump Sets, Gujarat. ~14,000 MSMEs district-wide, of which ~5,500 are engineering-specific, ~700 are foundry units, ~110 are investment casting units, and ~160 are auto component units. Anchor firms include Jyoti CNC Automation, Macpower CNC Machines, Falcon Pumps, Angel Pumps, and Fidel Pumps. The apex body, the Rajkot Engineering Association (REA), was incorporated in 1963 and now has over 1,600 member units. A Common Facility Centre for testing pump sets and foundry materials has institutionalised shared quality infrastructure across the cluster [9].
The internal supply chain is the interconnectedness story. Rajkot's roughly 700 grey iron foundries are internal raw-material suppliers to the pump-set cluster within the same district, supplying cast iron and SG iron bodies, impeller castings, and volute casings directly to Falcon Pumps, Angel Pumps, Fidel Pumps and other assemblers. Bearings, a critical sub-component in every pump and machine tool, are not made in Rajkot itself but sourced from the Vitthal Udyognagar/Wadhwan bearing cluster elsewhere in Gujarat, which also supplies Ahmedabad pump manufacturers (Lubi Industries, La Gajjar) and Coimbatore-based pump and motor manufacturers in Tamil Nadu. Per SAMEEEKSHA's and DC-MSME's Rajkot cluster profiles, virtually the entire pump value chain operates within the Rajkot-Gujarat belt: raw material procurement, foundry/casting, machining, motor procurement (partly sourced from Coimbatore), assembly and testing at the CFC, and dispatch, largely without leaving the region except for the bearing and motor inputs [8].

The compiled Cluster Summary Scorecard currently records the Rajkot Engineering Cluster's linkage verdict as Thin-Agglomeration Only, with one firm logged and one agglomeration-only linkage confirmed so far against zero confirmed transaction linkages [9], a lower confidence rating than the rich internal-supply-chain narrative above would suggest, reflecting that this is based on limited fieldwork evidence logged in the linkage map to date rather than a claim that the linkages described above are unconfirmed. The related Rajkot Electric Motor entry is rated Partially Linked, sourcing from foundries locally and nationally, with some output reportedly shipping to the Coimbatore cluster, though the client-side linkage is less documented than the sourcing side [9]. Closing this fieldwork-to-database gap, formally logging the transaction linkages the narrative evidence already describes, is a natural next step for the ongoing capstone fieldwork on this cluster.

For comparative context, four other Engineering Goods clusters are registered nationally: Coimbatore (Tamil Nadu, rank 2, spanning pump, motor & pump, and light-engineering sub-clusters worth ₹5,000 crore/₹2,150 crore/₹300 crore respectively), Bengaluru's Peenya Industrial Area (Karnataka, rank 3, India's largest machine-tool cluster at roughly 60% of national machine-tool production by value), Pimpri-Chinchwad (Maharashtra, rank 4, OEM-anchored with Kirloskar, Atlas Copco, Thermax, Forbes Marshall), and Faridabad (Haryana, rank 5, ~12,000 MSMEs, ₹1,06,668 crore turnover) [9]. A documented cross-cluster linkage also exists within Maharashtra: Kolhapur Steel foundry (part of the Kirloskar group) supplies large steel castings to Bhosari/Kirloskarvadi pump assembly, a within-state pattern structurally similar to Rajkot's foundry-to-assembler linkage.

Summary

Engineering Goods and Machinery presents a genuinely different competitiveness profile from the Electronics sector snapshot. Where Electronics scales fast but captures a shrinking share of its own output value, Engineering Goods grows more slowly (GVO +181% vs. Electronics' +331%) but holds its value-added intensity essentially flat, stays Competitive rather than swinging through a Highly Concentrated phase, and shows a firm leaderboard dominated by the same established capital-goods manufacturers across the decade rather than a wave of new low-margin assemblers. The unresolved problem is trade, not domestic value capture: a ₹1.95 lakh crore deficit concentrated in one HS category (machinery not elsewhere classified) sourced overwhelmingly from one country (China, 43.7% of imports), and traceable to a specific, well-documented capability gap in precision machine tools that the sector's overwhelmingly MSME-scale, capital-constrained firm base has not yet closed. The Rajkot cluster is a working example of what this sector does well, deep, genuine internal supply-chain integration across foundries, bearings, and assembly, even though the formal linkage evidence logged in the cluster register currently lags well behind the richness of the fieldwork narrative describing it.
